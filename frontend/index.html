How to use Race Analysis

1. Load a weekend. Choose a season and race at the top, then select a session from the left rail.

2. Read the session first. Classification, position, pace, sectors, tyres and pit stops describe the whole field.

3. Compare two laps. In Telemetry Detail, choose the reference first and the lap you want to compare against it second.

Delta rule. A positive delta means the compared driver took longer than the reference; a negative delta means the compared driver was quicker.

How comparison works

The reference driver is the baseline. The dashboard aligns both selected laps by distance so braking, speed, throttle, gear and elapsed-time differences can be compared at the same place on track. Use Validity and Confidence before drawing a conclusion: different tyres, weather, traffic or sessions reduce how directly the laps can be compared.

    Lower is better

    Competitive performance

    Pace separated from the final result.

    Qualifying deficit

    How to read: the gap to the qualifying benchmark at each round. At 0% the entry matches the fastest reference; 1% means roughly one second per 100-second lap slower. Lower is better. A rolling window smooths short-term variation.

    Race-pace deficit

    How to read: the median gap in representative race laps after data-quality exclusions. Lower is faster. It estimates sustained pace rather than using the single fastest lap, so traffic, tyres and conditions still matter.

    Qualifying versus race pace

    How to read: each point is a team. Further left means stronger qualifying pace; lower means stronger race pace. The ideal area is bottom-left. Distance from the diagonal reveals teams that perform relatively better in one session type.

    Pace rank → finish rank

    How to read: compare estimated race-pace rank with the official finishing position. A better finish than pace rank can suggest strong execution or race circumstances; a worse finish can reflect incidents, reliability or strategy. Clean laps and confidence show evidence quality.

    Relative, circuit-normalized pace

    Car development and change points

    Whether relative pace is improving or declining over time.

    Development table: Rate is the fitted percentage change per round; a negative rate means the deficit is shrinking and therefore improving. Early Q and Recent Q compare the first and latest three events. Change is recent minus early, so negative is improvement.

    Change points: these cards flag unusually large shifts in the pace series. “Before” and “after” are the estimated levels around that round; association does not prove an upgrade caused the change.

    Upgrade associations

    How to read: sourced upgrade reports are listed beside nearby performance changes. Confidence describes the evidence match, not certainty that the component caused the result.

    Team comparison and track suitability

    Relative speed, execution and circuit fit.

    Circuit-type strength

    How to read: each value compares a team with its own normal season pace on that circuit type. Negative means stronger than its usual level; positive means weaker. Use the event count and confidence before comparing cells.

    Performance-to-points conversion

    How to read: expected points are estimated from pace rank; actual points are what the team scored. Points above the equal-performance line indicate more points than the pace model expected; points below indicate fewer. This measures outcomes, not strategy quality alone.

    Team table: lower qualifying and race deficits are faster; higher reliability is better. Pts vs expected is actual minus model-estimated points, while Momentum is a 0–100 recent-form index. These measures answer different questions and should not be added together.

    Driver comparison and consistency

    How to read: Qualifying H2H is sessions won over comparable rounds. A negative teammate delta means the driver was quicker. Lower race-pace deficit is faster; lower Consistency MAD means pace varied less between events. Comparable laps and Confidence show how much evidence supports the result.
